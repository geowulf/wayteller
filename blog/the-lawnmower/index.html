From the Front Seat — No. 1

A Lawnmower, Not a Car

A reserved ride to the airport turned into a conversation about the worst birthday gift a dad ever gave his kid — and why it might be the best one.

By Greg Woolf · October 9, 2026

The pickup was a reserved ride to the airport — a man headed out on American, first time he'd ever reserved a car the night before. We left right on time, and somewhere between his street and the freeway we got to talking, the way you do.

I told him the truth about long rides: I don't remember them for the miles. I remember them for the conversation. The silent ones — those are the long ones, even when they're twenty minutes.

We got onto kids. Phones, mostly. He's got grandkids; I've got a sixteen-year-old. Somewhere in there I said what I say to a lot of riders: this generation isn't lazy, it's device-raised — and a device never once asked a kid, "What are you good for?"

Which reminded me of a story I've carried around ever since I heard a man tell it — and I've told it in this car more times than I can count.

He said people kept asking him about the most surprising gift he'd ever gotten, and his answer was: the worst gift his father ever gave him. Sixteenth birthday. All week the boy is sure he's getting a car — I mean, he's sure. Dad takes him outside, big grin, throws up the garage door… and there sits a brand-new lawnmower. Ribbon on it and everything.

The kid goes, "Why would you do that to me?"

And his dad goes, "Hear me out. This is a device that can give you the opportunity to make unlimited amounts of money. Learn what it teaches you — resourcefulness, showing up, doing it right — and I guarantee you, you will end up with a better car than the one you're dreaming about."

So the kid went to work. First by the job. Then by the hour. Then he hired his friends and let them do the work while he ran the thing. He said he did the math in his twenties, just to see: that lawnmower had earned him $120,000.

He goes, "My dad wasn't wrong."

I tell that story because it's how it works at my house. My son Jack mows the lawn — and then I inspect it. That's the deal. You mow the lawn, I inspect it. If it passes, he gets the trimmer — more work, more money. But nobody touches the trimmer until the lawn is right.

Not long ago he came to me wanting a couple of dollars, and I asked him the question nobody had ever asked him: "What do you have of value that I want — that I can give you two dollars for?" He struggled. And he's not a lazy kid; he'd just never had to say it out loud. That's the whole thing right there. One day somebody in a job interview is going to ask him what value he brings, and half the room is going to describe the job back to the interviewer — because all they know is what the job pays them.

The mower man understood it at sixteen because his dad made him look. Most kids never get the chance.

We talked about the other side of it too — the neighbor side. I told him about Pie Night. Every year, the night before Thanksgiving, we invite the whole neighborhood over. Everybody brings exactly one pie. That's the whole rule: one pie.

The first year in our neighborhood, my kids and I walked the street knocking on doors with flyers, and one man opened his door, listened, and goes, "You're weird."

I said, "Thank you. You're still invited."

He goes, "People don't do that anymore." And I said, "At least you said anymore. People used to. Maybe we're just bringing it back." He told me he thought it was dangerous — inviting strangers in. These are his neighbors. I said, "People don't care about their neighbors." And that's true of most streets. We do.

My wife does the same thing in restaurants — she carries a bag of games, and if there's a wait, we play. Strangers lean in. Some of them have ended up at our table, and a few have ended up at our house for dinner and a game. People say nobody talks to anybody anymore. In my car, they do. At my table, they do.

The man made his flight. I don't know if he'll remember the ride. But I'll remember the conversation, because it put the two halves of my life in one front seat: teach a kid to make value, and teach a street to show up. Both of them start with somebody deciding people are worth the effort.

Here's what I know: a lawnmower is only a bad gift if nobody teaches you what it's for. Same with a kid. Same with a neighborhood. The gift was never the mower.

The gift was a dad who could see what it would make of him.
